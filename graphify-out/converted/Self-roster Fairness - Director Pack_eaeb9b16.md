How should we run self-rostering? — Your vote


A few of you have said the self-roster round feels like a race — whoever logs in first gets their preferred shifts, and anyone on leave, or flat-out clinically when it opens picks from what's left. Fair call, and it deserves a group decision, not a change imposed from above.
The one guarantee first: Everyone's quota and the coverage rules stay exactly the same in every option — this decides only HOW shifts are chosen, never how many anyone works.
How this works: Read the five options below (including keeping what we have), then vote at the link in the email — under two minutes, anonymous, one response per person — pick your top two options. The two most popular options go to a quick head-to-head, and the winner runs for ONE roster cycle as a trial. If we don't like it, we revert. Nothing is permanent.
Option A — Keep it as is: open booking (first tap wins)
How it works: The round opens and everyone books instantly on the live roster — a taken slot disappears for everyone else. Quotas cap how many shifts anyone can take, and extra slots only open up once minimum cover is filled.
- Good: instant certainty — you pick it, it's yours; nothing new to learn; the roster fills fast.
- Consider: speed decides who gets the nicer shifts; if you're post-nights or away when it opens, you choose from leftovers; the same quick people tend to win every cycle.
Option B — Turn-based booking (rotating order)
How it works: Everyone books in a published order that changes each cycle (for example a reversing “snake” order, or random groups going in waves). You book when your turn or group window comes up.
- Good: no scramble at opening time; the order visibly rotates, so early advantage evens out across cycles; you still choose your own shifts.
- Consider: you must act when your turn arrives (awkward if you're on a clinical shift); a full round takes days; within a group window it can still feel quick-fingered.
Option C — Preference week + fair draw
How it works: The round stays open a full week — submitting on day 1 or day 7 makes no difference. For each day you mark AM, PM, “prefer not”, or UA (a day you can't work — everyone has a fair UA allowance scaled to their shifts), with backups if you like. At the deadline: if you're the only one asking for a session, it's yours. If more people want a session than it holds, a recorded draw decides — and anyone who loses a day goes to the front of the queue for the next draw, even later that same round. UA days are never overridden.
- Good: no race and no racing feeling — a week to answer whenever suits; most requests don't clash and are simply granted; if you miss out, the system owes you next time automatically; every allocation has a one-line explanation.
- Consider: results arrive at the deadline rather than instantly; contested sessions mean someone visibly misses the day they wanted (but gains priority); a small new habit to learn.
Option D — Points budget (bid for what matters)
How it works: Your points budget follows your contracted hours (about one point per rosterable hour — e.g. 76 points full-time, 56 at 0.7 EFT). You spend it on the shifts or days you care most about; highest points win a contested slot, and losing bids are refunded in full.
- Good: lets you say HOW MUCH something matters — a family event can outrank a mild preference; no speed involved; budgets scale with your hours, so everyone holds the same power per shift.
- Consider: colleagues end up bidding against each other for personal events; rewards tactical play; losing a slot you bid heavily on stings; hardest to explain and run.
Option E — Preferences in, roster out (drafted for you)
How it works: Everyone submits availability and preferences by a deadline. The roster is then drafted to be as fair as possible — spreading popular and unpopular shifts evenly within all the rules — and you fine-tune afterwards by swapping.
- Good: no race, no draws, nobody visibly loses a head-to-head; the good-and-bad spread is balanced by design; least ongoing effort.
- Consider: you receive a roster rather than choose it; explaining any single allocation is harder; least sense of personal control.
At a glance

What happens next: vote at the link in the email. Top two options go to a quick head-to-head the following week; the winner trials for one cycle with an open review. Use the comment box freely — especially for any fairer idea we haven't thought of; a strong idea joins the shortlist. Questions or dealbreakers — grab me anytime.

Our roster, our say — thanks for voting.
For you as Director — recommendation, process, guardrails
Recommendation. Option C (preference week + fair draw) fits our group best: it removes both the race and the feeling of one, everyone still chooses their own days, it's explainable in one breath, and it self-corrects — whoever misses a contested day holds priority for the next. Option D (points budget) is my close second — it runs on the same engine as C (same sealed week, same draws, same carry-over) and adds the ability to put extra weight on the days that matter most; right for us if the group wants that control and accepts the strategy it demands. Option B can be introduced immediately with what we already have, as a stopgap if the group wants relief sooner.
Process. All five options go to staff as a two-round anonymous vote (Microsoft Forms, one response per person): Round 1 picks the top two, Round 2 is a head-to-head. The winner runs for ONE roster cycle as an explicit trial, then we review it together against staff sentiment and an equity summary of how the weekends, evenings and on-call actually fell across the group. If it doesn't earn its place, we revert.
Guardrails. No option changes quotas, coverage minimums, safe-hours rules, and none carries a cost. Options A and B need nothing new; Option C needs modest setup; D and E are larger undertakings.

Self-rostering review · July 2026
Self-roster fairness — Option A explained
Open booking (today's system) · how it works in detail
In one sentence: The roster opens at a published time and you book your own shifts directly — first tap holds the slot, and booking takes seconds.
The timeline each cycle
1.  Booking opens — at a published date and time, announced to everyone.
2.  You book — a live view shows every day; a slot with space can be taken instantly, and a full slot closes.
3.  Booking stays open — for days or weeks; you can adjust your own picks while slots remain.
4.  Gap fill — unfilled slots are settled with the group — asks, swaps, adjustments.
5.  Roster published
How booking works
- Live availability — each period has a staffing minimum and maximum; when the maximum is reached the slot closes.
- Instant certainty — a booked slot is yours the moment you tap it — no waiting for any result.
- Your quota — you book up to your usual EFT-based number of shifts; leave is blocked out as now.
- Days you don't want — you simply don't book them; whatever is left over is resolved at gap-fill.
Worked example
Saturday 15th, PM, 2 slots — Alex, Sam, Kim and Jo all want it. Booking opens 9:00am. Alex books at 9:00:12, Sam at 9:00:40 — the slot is now full. Kim looks at 9:05 and Jo that evening: both see it closed. Nothing decided that outcome except speed.
What this means day-to-day
- Be online at opening — or the popular slots are gone — this is the “race” the group has raised.
- Away at opening time — on a clinical shift, on leave, or simply busy — you pick from leftovers.
- No memory — missing out this cycle earns you nothing next cycle; the same people can lose repeatedly.
Note: colleagues on fixed patterns are unaffected under this option — their shifts are entered first, exactly as now, and the process above applies only to flexible staff, over the slots that remain.
The takeaway
This is the simplest possible system: instant, transparent in the moment, and no waiting. Its weakness is exactly the complaint on the table — contested slots go to the fastest, every cycle, with no compensation. The vote is about whether that trade is still acceptable.
Self-roster fairness — Option B explained
Turn-based picking · how it works in detail
In one sentence: Everyone picks their shifts in a published order, one person at a time — and the order rotates every cycle, so nobody is permanently early or permanently late.
The timeline each cycle
1.  Pick order published — rotated from last cycle: whoever went first moves to the back, everyone else moves up one.
2.  Turns run — each person gets a pick window (say 24 hours) to book their shifts, then the next person's turn begins. You're notified when your window opens.
3.  Rounds (the fairer variant) — rather than booking everything on one turn, picking runs in rounds — say 2 shifts per round — with the order reversing each round (1→12, then 12→1), so early pickers go late in the next round.
4.  Clean-up — missed turns and remaining slots are settled at the end; swaps as usual.
5.  Roster published
How a turn works
- Your window is yours alone — no racing — within it you pick at leisure from whatever is still open.
- Rotation is the fairness engine — across cycles everyone passes through both the front and the back of the queue.
- Miss your turn — you're skipped, not punished — pick later from what's left, or ask for help placing your shifts.
Worked example
Saturday 15th, PM, 2 slots — Alex, Sam, Kim and Jo all want it. This cycle's order happens to place Sam 2nd, Jo 5th, Alex 8th, Kim 11th. Sam books it on turn 2; Jo on turn 5 — full. Alex and Kim never get the chance this cycle. Next cycle the order rotates and they sit nearer the front.
The honest edges
- Elapsed time — 12 people × 24-hour windows is nearly two weeks of turns; shorter windows speed it up but squeeze people who are away or on clinical shifts.
- Losing still happens — rotation compensates across cycles, not within one — the back of this cycle's queue still watches good slots disappear.
- Your turn can land badly — on leave or on a clinical shift when your window opens, you must plan ahead or delegate — protection is only partial.
- Someone has to chase — turns stall when people forget; the process needs nudging to keep moving.
Note: colleagues on fixed patterns are unaffected under this option — their shifts are entered first, exactly as now, and the process above applies only to flexible staff, over the slots that remain.
The takeaway
Turn-based kills the sprint and is easy to explain in one line. Its costs are calendar time — the roster takes weeks to settle — and the fact that being late in this cycle's order still hurts, with the make-good arriving only next cycle.
Self-roster fairness — Option C explained
Preference week + draw · how it works in detail
In one sentence: Everyone marks the days they'd like to work during a set week, then slots are handed out all at once — uncontested picks go straight through, contested ones are decided by an open draw, and anyone who loses a draw gets priority next time. Speed never matters.
The timeline each cycle
1.  Preference week opens — about 4 weeks before the cycle starts, open for 7 days. Mark your preferences whenever suits you — marking on Monday morning or Friday night makes zero difference, because nothing is allocated until the window closes.
2.  Window closes — no more changes.
3.  Allocation day — the sorting and draws run all at once, in date order. Results and draw records are published the same day.
4.  Review window — check what you got; swaps and adjustments as usual.
5.  Roster published
What you mark during preference week
- AM or PM — “I'd like to work this period this day.” Marking both on the same day means “either suits me” — you'll never be given both (no double shifts).
- Prefer not — “I'd rather not, if it can be helped.” Unlimited and free — the engine avoids these days wherever it can, though one may be used if a day would otherwise gap. Save UA for genuine cannots; prefer-not covers everything else.
- Cannot work (UA) — “I cannot work this day.” Never overridden once accepted — not by the draw, not by auto-fill — and it doesn't reduce your shift count. Each cycle has a visible UA budget scaled to your load: about one cannot per three shifts you owe, rounded up, minimum 6 (e.g. 28 shifts → 10; 21 → 7). It adjusts automatically if leave reduces your shifts that cycle. The cap is what guarantees an accepted UA is never broken. Leave is separate and doesn't touch the budget.
- Blank — “no strong feeling.” Blank days are where your remaining shifts are placed.
- On-call flag — tick if you're willing to take on-call. Actual leave still goes through leave requests as now — leave and UA are different things.
Three kinds of slot
- Uncontested — the people who wanted it fit the slots available. Everyone gets it — no draw, no points involved. Most slots land here.
- Contested — more people wanted it than there are slots. Decided by the draw below.
- Unclaimed — nobody marked it. Filled automatically from people's blank days, spread evenly and steered by the equity picture, always avoiding UA days.
The draw — exact rules
1.  Priority points win first — everyone carries a points balance (most sit at 0). Candidates are ranked by points, highest first — anyone clear of the pack takes a slot before any randomness happens.
2.  Ties are drawn by lot — whoever is left tied for the remaining slots goes into a random draw — the electronic equivalent of names out of a hat.
3.  Every draw leaves a receipt — the record shows the slot, who entered, everyone's points at entry, and the result — visible to all. Nobody has to trust it was fair; anyone can look.
4.  Win a contested slot — (by points or by luck) you get the shift and your points reset to 0.
5.  Lose a draw — +1 point, usable immediately — starting from the very next contested draw processed (draws run in date order).
Carry-over — why the luck evens out
The points are the system's memory. Losing a draw is only ever a delay, not a loss:
- Lose a draw → +1 point → you now outrank everyone at 0 in the next contested slot you've marked.
- The point doesn't expire. If nothing else is contested this cycle, it carries into the next cycle.
- Points reset only when they've done their job — when you win a contested slot. Uncontested slots never touch your points.
So whoever has the worst luck this cycle is, by construction, first in line next time. Over a few cycles everyone's share of the popular slots evens out — without anyone having to negotiate it.
Worked example
Week one: Saturday 15th, PM, 2 slots. Marked by Alex (0 pts), Sam (0 pts), Kim (0 pts) and Jo (1 pt — lost a draw last Tuesday).
- Jo holds the highest points → takes slot 1 automatically, and resets to 0.
- Alex, Sam and Kim are tied at 0 for the last slot → random draw → Sam wins, stays at 0.
- Alex and Kim each get +1.
Week two: Saturday 22nd, PM, 2 slots. Marked by Alex (1), Kim (1) and Sam (0).
- Alex and Kim outrank Sam → they take both slots and reset to 0. Sam misses out → +1.
- If Sam contests nothing else this cycle, that point carries into next cycle's first contested draw.
Nobody queued, nobody raced, and the record shows exactly why each person got what they got.
What a receipt looks like
Every placement on the roster carries its reason — not just the draws. Examples:
- Uncontested — “Sat 15 PM: 2 slots, 2 requests → granted.”
- Won on priority — “3 requests, 2 slots. You entered with 1 point → placed first; points reset to 0.”
- Won by draw — “Tied at 0 for the last slot → audited draw → you won.” The record keeps who entered, points at entry, and the draw seed and time.
- Lost a draw — “Draw went against you → +1 priority from your next contested day; balance now 1.”
- Auto-filled — “You owed 3 more shifts; Tue 19 AM chosen from your blank days to meet staffing and balance your tough-shift share.”
- Prefer-not used (rare) — “Wed 20 was ‘prefer not’, but the day would have gapped and you were the only eligible colleague under target.”
- Zone steering — “Placed in Paeds to keep your 50% target on track (currently 42%).”
Receipts are visible to everyone, draws are seeded so a result can be re-checked, and the log is append-only — the answer to “why didn't I get the 15th?” is the same record a month later.
The edge rules, stated once
- One clinical period per day, maximum — win the AM and you're withdrawn from that day's PM pool automatically.
- Zones — you pick the day and period; the system balances zones across the cycle — no zone-camping.
- Missed the window? — you're treated as all-blank: your shifts are auto-filled fairly around your leave, and you can swap afterwards as usual. No penalty — but no picks either.
- Quotas don't change — same number of shifts per person as today. This changes how the popular days are shared, not how much anyone works.
- UA coverage floor — a day stops accepting UA once the eligible pool would drop to minimum staffing plus a margin (zone-aware, so scarce skills stay protected). If more people seek UA on a day than it can afford, it's decided by the draw and carry-over at window close — never by who marked first.
- After publication — nothing changes — swaps, leave and the rest of the process stay exactly as they are now.
Zones and special roles
The ballot stays about days — nobody marks zones. Each person carries an admin-set profile: which zones they can work, and any percentage target (e.g. “about half my time in Paeds”).
1.  Capacity is zone-by-zone underneath — “Saturday AM, 4 slots” is really Paeds 1 + Resus 1 + Acute 2. Staff who work anywhere never notice; a Paeds-only colleague effectively contests only the Paeds slot of the days they mark.
2.  Most-constrained placed first — which dissolves most clashes: if Priya (Paeds-only) and Marcus (50% Paeds) both mark Saturday AM, Priya takes Paeds and Marcus works Acute — no draw. A real draw happens only between two Paeds-only colleagues on the same day, decided by the normal rules — points and carry-over unchanged.
3.  Percentage targets balance across the cycle — about half of Marcus's shifts are steered into Paeds over the weeks, visible as a running tally — never forced day by day.
4.  Auto-fill respects eligibility — Paeds gaps fill only from Paeds-eligible blank days; impossible cover is flagged to the admin, never quietly given to someone unqualified.
5.  Scarce-skill guardrail — if only two consultants cover Paeds, the system warns before both go unavailable the same day. Draw records show the zone scope (“Sat 15 AM — Paeds, 1 slot”), so nobody wonders why a colleague “wasn't in the draw”.
Fixed patterns and flexible staff
Some colleagues work fixed patterns; the rest self-roster. Option C keeps that structure exactly as it is — the fixed layer runs first, and preference week applies only to flexible staff.
1.  Fixed patterns stamped first — before preference week opens, fixed-shift colleagues are already on the roster, as now. No marking, no draws, no points — nothing changes for them at all.
2.  Flexible staff see what remains — each day offers the staffing maximum minus fixed colleagues already placed. A day the fixed layer fills completely shows as full during preference week, so nobody wastes a mark — or enters a draw — for a slot that doesn't exist.
3.  Points are a flexible-staff ledger — only draw entrants accrue carry-over. A fixed pattern's trade-off — certainty instead of choice — was settled when the pattern was agreed. The equity view still counts everyone's tough shifts.
4.  Leave and hybrids — a fixed colleague's leave returns those slots to the pool. Someone fixed for part of the week goes through preference week for the rest of their quota, with UA and preferences applying to that flexible remainder.
The takeaway
Today, entry speed decides the good shifts. Under Option C, three things decide instead: your stated preferences, your priority points, and — only for ties — an auditable draw. The honest trade-off: you can want a slot, enter the draw, and not get it that cycle. The carry-over point is the compensation, and it is automatic and visible.
Self-roster fairness — Option D explained
Points budget (bidding) · how it works in detail
In one sentence: Your points budget follows your contracted hours — one point per rosterable hour — and you spread it across the days you want. When a day is oversubscribed, the higher bids win, and losing bids are refunded in full.
The timeline each cycle
1.  Bidding week opens — about 4 weeks before the cycle, open 7 days. Bids are sealed — nobody sees anyone else's, and timing within the week is irrelevant.
2.  Window closes — no more changes.
3.  Resolution day — every slot resolves at once; results and the bid records published the same day.
4.  Review window — check what you got; swaps as usual.
5.  Roster published
How bidding works
1.  Your budget — 1 point per rosterable contracted hour per fortnight — e.g. 76 points at full-time, 56 at 0.7 EFT — pro-rated if you're on leave part of the cycle. This equalises power where it counts: everyone holds about the same points per shift they owe (76 across ~19 shifts ≈ 56 across ~14 ≈ 4 per shift), so no EFT out-muscles another.
2.  Place your bids — split the budget across the day-and-period slots you want — 40 on a must-have Saturday, 5 each on nice-to-haves. Total bids can't exceed your budget. “Prefer not” and capped “cannot” (UA) days work exactly as in Option C — free, and never paid for: bids protect wants; a genuine cannot is never something you bid to avoid.
3.  Uncontested slots — fewer bidders than slots → everyone gets in and every bid is refunded. No contest, no cost.
4.  Contested slots — highest bids win; winners pay what they bid; losers are refunded in full. Equal bids — and slots where every marker spent zero — are decided by the audited draw with carry-over priority, exactly as in Option C. Nothing, anywhere, is decided by who applied first.
5.  Carry-over — unspent and refunded points roll into next cycle, capped at about 1.5× a cycle's budget — sitting out a popular Saturday buys strength later, without letting anyone hoard a super-budget.
Worked example
Saturday 15th, PM, 2 slots — Alex bids 30, Sam 25, Kim 10, Jo 5. Alex and Sam win and pay their bids; Kim and Jo are refunded. Next month a long-weekend Saturday comes up: Kim, who has been saving, bids 45 and wins comfortably over bidders who spent early.
The edge rules, stated once
- Blank days — anything you didn't bid on or mark UA is where your remaining shifts are auto-filled, spread fairly.
- Quotas don't change — bids decide which days you work, never how many.
- One clinical period per day — win the AM and your PM bid for that day is refunded automatically.
- Zones — balanced by the system across the cycle, as in every option.
Note: colleagues on fixed patterns are unaffected under this option — their shifts are entered first, exactly as now, and the process above applies only to flexible staff, over the slots that remain.
The takeaway
This is the most expressive option — you can say how much a day matters, not just that it matters. Spend nothing everywhere and it behaves exactly like Option C; its extra power is that a big bid can manufacture near-certainty for the one shift that truly matters that cycle. The price is strategy: you're estimating what others will bid, and misjudging means overpaying or missing out. Some will enjoy that game; others will resent having to play it to get a Saturday.
Self-roster fairness — Option E explained
Drafted for you · how it works in detail
In one sentence: You tell the system your preferences once; it drafts the entire roster in a single pass, balancing everyone's wishes against the fairness record; you review, swap and publish.
The timeline each cycle
1.  Preference week — mark your days exactly as in Option C: AM/PM likes, unlimited “prefer not” days, and capped “cannot work” (UA) days with the same visible budget and zone-aware coverage floor — plus standing preferences such as “prefer mornings”, “cluster my shifts together” or “spread them out”.
2.  Window closes — no more changes.
3.  Draft day — the engine builds the whole roster for everyone at once.
4.  Draft published with explanations — you see what you got and why; review and swap window follows.
5.  Roster published
How the engine decides
1.  Hard rules first — capped UA days, leave, staffing numbers and safe-hours rules are never broken — they are walls, not preferences.
2.  Preference score — the draft aims to give each person the highest possible share of their marked days — avoiding prefer-not days wherever it can — with everyone weighted equally.
3.  Contested days go to the ledger — when more people want a day than there are slots, two observable facts decide — no scores, no opinions: who has missed the most marked days recently (the same carry-over idea as Option C's priority points), and who carries the higher tough-shift share. The others' ledgers are credited — compensation is built into the engine.
4.  Everything is explained — each person sees a summary like “you received 9 of your 11 marked days; Saturday 15th went to a colleague who had missed more marked days recently.”
Worked example
Saturday 15th, PM, 2 slots — Alex, Sam, Kim and Jo all marked it. The engine consults the ledger: Jo missed the most marked days last cycle, and Kim currently carries the highest tough-shift share — they get the two slots. Alex and Sam are credited, which puts them first in line at the next contested day they've marked.
The edge rules, stated once
- Quotas don't change — the engine fills your usual EFT-based number of shifts, never more.
- One clinical period per day — never both, and never on a UA day.
- Zones — balanced automatically across the cycle.
- After the draft — swaps work exactly as now — the draft is a starting point, not a verdict.
Note: colleagues on fixed patterns are unaffected under this option — their shifts are entered first, exactly as now, and the process above applies only to flexible staff, over the slots that remain.
The takeaway
This option asks the least of you — mark your days once and receive a roster that has weighed every trade-off at once. What you give up is watching the decision happen: even with clear explanations, the deciding lives inside an algorithm, and “the computer chose” sits more comfortably with some people than others. The input is easy; the output takes trust.
